Ludd and Guts

Black Skin, Metal Masks

by Lovebug Gramski

The last thing to fit was the mask. Would it conform to my twisted features in comfort?
(?, Operation:Doomsday)

It is seldom remarked that masks are absent from Frantz Fanon’s Black Skin, White Masks. Other than the title, the word ‘masque’ is used twice in the original French, both times to refer to the masking of something, and is duly rendered as ‘conceal’ and ‘closing our eyes to’ in English translations. Perhaps it is too obvious to need remark that the mask of the title represents the white ideals that Black people must appropriate in order to operate in the white world, and this interpretation could be evidenced throughout the book. However, because ‘white masks’ go unmentioned, are plural in the title, and because Fanon doesn’t directly say how they relate to ‘Black skin’, there is some liberty in how to interpret them.

After the release of the album Operation:Doomsday in 1999, Daniel Dumile performed in a metal mask in the style of Marvel villain Dr. Doom. While he spoke briefly about the meaning of the mask in interviews, it is only mentioned in his lyrics twice on this album: when he describes himself as ‘metalface’ on Gas Drawls, and on the song ? when he says ‘he cleans his metal mask with gasoline’. It is spoken of by analogy in the skits, which tell the origin story of Dr. Doom’s mask, but it is otherwise conspicuously absent.

Both Fanon and Dumile leave the meaning of their masks up for interpretation. I aim in this essay to discover their masks anew and to attempt to reveal their expansiveness.

And then the occasion arose when I had to meet the white man’s eyes. An unfamiliar weight burdened me.
(The Fact of Blackness)

Mr. Hood: My name is Mr. Hood. What is your name?
Zev Love X: Hmm, yeah I’m Zev Love X from KMD.
Mr. Hood: I am pleased to meet you.
(Mr. Hood at Piocalle’s Jewelry/Crackpot, Mr. Hood)

Prior to Operation:Doomsday, Dumile released two albums as part of the group KMD. The first of these, Mr. Hood, is structured around three encounters between the eponymous Mr. Hood and the members of KMD: Daniel Dumile AKA Zev Love X meets Mr. Hood overspending on overpriced jewellery, then Alonzo Hodge AKA Onyx meets Mr. Hood at a house party playing the dozens, and finally Dumile’s brother Dingilizwe Dumile AKA Subroc cuts Mr. Hood’s hair. Mr. Hood is a local drug dealer and walking stereotype of inner city Black america, but his voice is sampled from a 1961 teach-yourself-Spanish record with a speaker who talks in clear and enunciated American English: a voice that sounds overly formal, uptight, and white. The group could as easily have used a live vocal, like the characters of ‘Mr Piocalles’ and ‘Preacher Porkchop’ elsewhere on the album, but by coding Mr. Hood’s voice as white they imply that this one-dimensional stereotype is a product of whiteness. Mr. Hood comes off as rude and ignorant as he flouts the rules of Black social spaces – offering Zev Love X crack and work selling it, asking Onyx for alcohol despite him being Muslim, and smoking obnoxiously in Subroc’s barbers chair. The result is that a Black body is hewn crudely from white material, which then lumbers into the scène à faire of Black american social life and is immediately incongruous. Elsewhere, other white-originating Black stereotypes are deployed in the album’s skits: Amos ‘n’ Andy characters (specifically the blackface radio version) rudely demand haircuts from Subroc, and the white narrator from a Little Black Sambo storybook record starts to narrate Zev Love X’s life as if he were the Sambo character. Contemporary and historic white ideas of blackness invade the group’s social spaces and cumulatively contribute to an idea of what blackness is, which the group must then refute. Their refutation is themselves: young Black Muslims, respectable and ascetic, with claims to a deep history of Black civilisation.

Although his text is without ‘masques’, Fanon does use the term ‘imago’ to describe the ideas about blackness held by white europeans. The term is borrowed from Lacan, who uses it to describe the reductive images of other people that inform how everyone perceives identity. It is the Latin term for a death mask, apt due to its imperfect resemblance to the real living person, but stable and simplified enough for symbolic purposes. Whiteness’s imagos of blackness occupy Fanon and Dumile, but as they wrote half-a-century apart their era’s imagos of blackness differ – from cannibalism, tom-toms and slave ships to dope fiends, crack dealers, and gang violence. However, both writers are developing strategies of countering these imagos. Chapter five of Fanon’s text, The Fact of Blackness, is structured as a dialogue in which he argues against a white voice. The imago is felt by Fanon as an ‘unfamiliar weight’ attending the white gaze, which his white interlocutor nevertheless expects him to emulate. While in this text a white interlocutor creates its masks of blackness and presents them each in turn to Fanon, on KMD’s album Dumile’s interlocutor is not a white voice but the imago itself. It is personified, white in a mask of blackness, as it enters a jewellery shop to offer greetings.

‘Dirty N*****!’ or simply ‘Look, a Negro!’ (The Fact of Blackness)

In our logo you see us?/ Whoever said that coon was me?
(Who me?, Mr. Hood)

The fifth chapter of Black Skin, White Masks begins with Fanon sitting on a train and being pointed out by a white child, making Fanon suddenly see himself through the white gaze. This moment is really the culmination of many other moments of encounter with the white gaze, which he reports by quoting everyday comments that drip with implication, imposing notions of white superiority upon him. The chapter reverberates with voices, quoting from, it is assumed, occasions in Fanon’s life though they may equally be regarded as common enough sentiments to be taken as idiomatic white: ‘Look how handsome the negro is’, ‘Oh, I want you to meet my Black friend’, and so on. On the surface, cordial enough; beneath, dehumanising, sexualising, and deindividuating. The white gaze is attended by an imago of blackness that determines how the child and the other white voices conceptualise the encounter. There is violence in trying to make this mask fit, and Fanon proceeds to decode the othering behind these phrases, outlining their impact on his sense of self as a Black man who must contend with how he is seen.

Similarly, the second track on Mr. Hood, Who me?, is about being seen by the white gaze. Like Fanon’s chapter, this song is Dumile’s response to having a racialising comment thrown at him, with several slurs named as if to represent many such encounters. It is also a response to being mistaken for the group’s logo: a sambo figure with a red cross through it. Their logo can simultaneously be read as a caricature of blackness or as a white person in blackface, so to present such a figure with a red cross through it is an ambivalent image – a Jim Crow sign prohibiting Black people, or a sign prohibiting blackface and other misrepresentations of Black life. By deploying this image as their logo, the group invite the former interpretation, revealing that these historical images continue to be legible to the white gaze, but the album delivers the latter, denouncing the persistence of this imagery.

Unlike Fanon’s white interlocutor, whiteness is mostly presented on the album through the imagos whiteness produces, but it is also presented as a ’they’ peering into Black social life, flashing glances at the group and murmuring abuse. In the album’s wide cast of characters, there are no white ones, at least none that could be pointed to as epitomising any kind of whiteness. Even the word ‘white’ is not used. Instead, various epithets like ‘devil’, ‘wolf’, and ‘monkey man’ that subvert those directed at Black people are hurled back by the group so that even the group’s defence highlights white racism. Rather than a figure of whiteness, the album depicts the white gaze, its imaginings, and its effects on the world. To white listeners this serves as a warning to examine their own looking, and to not overdetermine the group from without. The album’s depiction of the white gaze anticipates the real white gaze in the group’s audience, and responds preemptively to its accusatory stares: ‘Who, me?’

I resolved to defend myself.
(The Fact of Blackness)

Mr. Hood: Show me something better.
(Mr. Hood at Piocalle’s Jewelry/Crackpot, Mr. Hood)

Having been presented with the imagos that attend the white gaze, both Fanon and Dumile deploy strategies to respond to them. Their first strategy is to discredit them by presenting an alternative, positive imago of blackness.

Fanon’s alternative imago finds its main source in the Négritude poets. From this movement’s proponents he finds a host of qualities from which to construct an alternative imago to show that blackness is superior to whiteness in some way. However, when he adopts these masks one after another against the white gaze, his white interlocutor dismisses them. Rhythm as a quality of blackness is offered first, punctured by its proximity to irrational carnality. Blackness’s intimacy with the earth is next, but the white voice points out the same quality in white mystics. Then Fanon appeals to Black African history, but this history cannot withstand the white gaze’s condescension; it responds, ‘we will turn to you as to the childhood of the world’. The final attempt is not to claim that blackness is superior to whiteness, but that the categories are inadequate because treated as absolutes. ‘There is not merely one negro, there are negroes’, Fanon argues, and so the fixed categories of Black and white are untenable. Whiteness is on the backfoot for a spell, but it is arighted by Sartre, who calls Black a ‘minor term’ to white in Orphée Noir. After Sartre delivers this final blow, Fanon ends the chapter with no alternative imago to hide behind.

Like Fanon, rhythm, earth-mysticism and history are the qualities the members of KMD offer as alternatives to the racist imagos put forth by whiteness. They are ‘crazy in tempo with the universe’, embody the ‘richness of earth’, and know that ‘we built this place’. These are how the group in skit and song rebut Mr. Hood, and show him a different way of being for Black men, informed by the Black Islamic spirituality and afrocentric positivity the group were known for at the time. Sartre’s ‘minor term’ also appears when Dumile refers to ‘Black cat bad luck theories’, quoting Michael Berrin AKA MC Serch. He is referring to the racist ideology that gives negative, inferior connotations to blackness and positive, superior connotations to whiteness. While Dumile’s alternative imagos are remarkably similar to Fanon’s, unlike Fanon’s they are not repudiated. It is clear, however, that KMD are on the defensive. As well as ’this place’ being ‘built’ by Black people, Dumile also explains that ’this place, he runs it’. Whether this ‘place’ is meant to refer to america, the world of the album, or the whole world, it is run by a ‘he’, being the white man whose supremacist ideology structures it. Dumile is formulating his alternative imago in a hostile, white ‘place’ besieged by racist tropes, making an alternative blackness hard to configure, and even harder to realise. Afrocentric positivity is the rebuttal on this album, released at a time when this formation dominated Hip Hop as epitomised by the success of The Zulu Nation and Native Tongues among others. However, its adequacy as a response to the white gaze is perhaps best gauged by the fact that this particular formation was abandoned by the group hereafter, as they and most of its other proponents concluded afrocentricity was of the past, and turned instead to images of life in america’s Black inner cities for inspiration instead.

I am a negro I am a negro I am a negro (The Fact of Blackness)

What a n**** know? Yo a n**** know the game.
(What a N**** know? BL_CK B_ST_RDS)

Having tried on alternative masks and found them wanting, Fanon relents to the child on the train. His incantatory ‘I am a negro’ yields to the child’s words, but it is ambiguous whether it is an annihilatory gesture or a violent one. His examples of what this means come from the Black-american novels Native Son and If he hollers let him go. Whether it is a killing spree or being hounded into exile, these books’ Black protagonists forsake their own will to do what the white world wants or expects them to, and for Fanon it is resorting to an effacing of the self that they have in common. With the inadequacy of these final models, Fanon concludes the chapter with one final strategy left him in response to the imago. He does not adopt it, instead, with nowhere to go, he weeps before it in defeat. The final mask of the chapter is one of capitulation.

KMD’s follow up, BL_CK B_ST_RDS, is the dark mirror image of Mr. Hood. This can be seen in specific lines, such as ‘[Zev] Love smokes no cancer sticks’ becoming ‘Smoking cancer sticks, and you don’t quit’. Whole songs also show such reversals: Peachfuzz on the first album is about how the group are viewed by women, whereas Plumskinzz reverses this gaze by being about how the group view women. The group’s respectable afrocentricity has also been replaced by smoking, drinking, gun-toting, womanizing, woman-beating caricatures. Where Mr. Hood responds to the white gaze with incredulity, BL_CK B_ST_RDS responds with cartoonish revelry, deploying all the devils of the white imaginary to confirm its most outlandish ideas of blackness. Incautious about how they may be perceived, the songs of the album run the gamut of controversial Hip Hop tropes. The piousness of the first album turns to hedonism; refuting the white gaze becomes performing to it. However, the tone is not the despair and capitulation that ends Fanon’s chapter. Rather it is a funhouse mirror of giddy irreverence.

The question both KMD albums seek to answer is – in the 90s how can Hip Hop respond to the white audience that is listening-in en-masse? The stage is mediated by whiteness and its imagos, false constructs of Black identity, so when Black artists take to this stage, are there ways to represent Black social life without it being co-opted, trivialised, or demonised? If not, what strategies can be deployed to undermine this gaze, or even to signify on it? The use of the n-word was central to this. A taboo among most white people, it is a provocation for white listeners to make them understand that the album is not for them, and the opening lines to What a n**** know? may have a few reaching furtively for the volume knob.

One is no longer aware of the Negro but only of a penis; the Negro is eclipsed. He is turned into a penis. He is a penis.
(The Negro and Psychopathology)

Alright come on blackie, what’re you hiding?
This, the dick!
(Garbage Day #3, BL_CK B_ST_RDS)

Although on its face the reproduction of negative stereotypes is simply validating the imagos of the white gaze, KMD offer them up to submit them to analysis. While Mr. Hood’s negative stereotypes of blackness are personified as characters and delegitimised by their origin in whiteness, BL_CK B_ST_RDS replaces these personifications of blackness with synecdoches of blackness, less masks than props – the gun (Get-U-Now), the cheap liquor (Sweet Premium Wine), the blunt (*Smokin’ that S#%**), and, indeed, ‘the dick’. Their odes to profligacy can be read as straight-faced, a reflection of their change in lifestyle now in their 20s and touring, but by separating the stereotypes into their constituent parts they now act as a taxonomy, analysing different preoccupations of Hip Hop at the time. Each are typical topics of chastisement whenever Hip Hop is in the headlines, but they also typify the counter-cultural excitement that the music offers white male youth. They have an attractive and repulsive aspect – inasmuch as they are controversial, they also provide a fantasy of emancipation from middle class ennui. They are an outlet for aggression towards teachers, bosses, democratic powerlessness, when you ask your mom please but she still says no. In this way, the white listeners’ love of gangster rap’s edgy tropes is a cry of impotence and displaced feeling.

When Fanon depicted his white interlocutor in chapter five, it spoke to him like an adult does to a child. As condescending as it was formidable, it used a grandiloquent rhetorical style to dismantle Fanon through forceful argument, reducing him to tears (a device of Fanon’s – he is speaking in both voices). This performance of an encounter establishes a grand image for whiteness, laying the groundwork for its dramatic peripeteia in the next chapter.

In The Negro and Psychopathology, Fanon moves this encounter to the chaise longue to analyse the symbolism that underlies the white imago of blackness. If blackness is a ‘minor term’ to whiteness, then when whiteness represents the mind and civilisation, what is left to blackness is the body and primitivism. To Fanon, what is common among Black stereotypes of athleticism, sexuality, savagery, cannibalism, or whatever is baseness and biology: ‘In relation [vis-à-vis] to the Negro, everything takes place on the genital level.’ To come to this conclusion, Fanon cites anecdotes, research data, case notes of white people undergoing psychoanalysis, and historic examples such as accounts of castration during lynchings. The overwhelming variety of this evidence gives credence to what could otherwise be dismissed as ridiculous.

Face to face [En face] with this man who is “different from himself,” he needs to defend himself. In other words, to personify The Other. The Other will become the mainstay of his preoccupations and his desires.

The white man’s ideas about blackness betray more of himself than he wants to admit. These ‘vis-à-vis’ or ’en face’ moments form a motif throughout the book and illustrate its title. With Black and white masked faces posed facing each other, the reader initially sees the unmoved and authoritative mask of whiteness looking condescendingly upon the imago of blackness. Fanon’s analysis makes the white man’s mask slip, and the reader is given a glimpse beneath at the sublimated thoughts contorting and agitating his expression. His preoccupations are not in fact civilisation and the intellect; his desires are not directed towards truth and enlightenment. Instead the reader sees the projections of a quivering insecurity that fetishises blackness, expressed in unconfessed sexual attraction, in an obsession with genitalia, in rape phobias, and in rape fantasies. The white man cuts a tragic figure because beneath his hubris are feelings of impotency, shame, and sexual inadequacy; a humiliating and perverted spectacle as pitiful as it is stupid. Fanon has figured out what lofty idea animates the white man: penis envy.

To us, the man who adores the Negro is as ‘sick’ as the man who abominates him. (Introduction)

Gotta be hard the way they master how to act Black. (Black Bastards!, BL_CK B_ST_RDS)

The title of BL_CK B_ST_RDS contains two puzzles. The first is obvious: what words do the missing letters reveal in the hangman game? The second is less straightforward: who does the phrase refer to? As with the logo, it invites the listener to imagine it is the group in their new hard rock personas, but they are quick to deny it: Zev Love X explicitly calls himself ‘A brown man, not a Black bastard’, and Subroc describes himself as ‘doo doo brown’ rather than Black. The song Black Bastards! provides the answer to both puzzles, which describes two occasions of whites imitating blackness. In its first verse, being greeted by a white man with, ‘what up Black?’ prompts Dumile to ask, ‘you, Black?’. The second verse describes Parker Lewis, a white sitcom actor, rapping onstage at Ed Lover’s birthday party. Dumile makes his own gaze explicit to this spectacle, giving Lewis a ‘beef beef look’ as he judges his rap as an ‘offbeat lick shot lick shot’, mocking Lewis’s poor imitation of Caribbean slang. On both occasions, Dumile remarks upon white people’s attempts to ‘act Black’, and at the end offers what they are missing:

See, I became underground since the life in the street
The love of the beat, large is the fleet
That will remain underground for all my boys whose souls sleep
Six feet deeper than the soles of my feet

Unlike the whites imitating blackness, Dumile has lived the subterranean survival of a Black man in a white supremacist society. It is real life that he appeals to and not the symbolic imago of blackness, positive or negative, he has offered elsewhere. It is here whites who are adopting a mask of blackness, but do so unconvincingly. In this way, the ‘Black bastards’ are the whites who have begun to crowd the scene, imitating the speech, art, and styles of the Black americans who originated them. These whites with their juvenile desires and pale imitations can be understood as bastardizing blackness.

Subroc my word’s bond and I’m gone
(Suspended Animation, BL_CK B_ST_RDS)

Before the album was completed, Dingilizwe Dumile was hit by a motorist and died. After the release of the album’s first single, the album was shelved because its cover was deemed too controversial by label heads and the group were dropped. The record ends with a promise from Daniel to his brother that the listener is not privy to.

Daniel Dumile’s ‘gone’ years have taken on a mythical status in his career, and with a new alias and style they could be treated as a moment of rebirth, but I would like to contend that what came afterwards can be viewed as an extension of what came before. While Dingilizwe’s death is necessarily central to any reading of Daniel Dumile’s follow-up record, I do not think that it should be solely attended to as an expression of grief. It is as much the next step in the brothers’ shared musical project of responding to whiteness.

None the less I am a man, and in this sense the Peloponnesian War is as much mine as the invention of the Compass.
(By Way of Conclusion)

On the slow-mo, the calm artist with the so-so chick,
Chased them all like how he did to Slobodan Milošević.
Anywho, how ‘bout them Yankees?
(I Hear Voices Part. 1, Operation:Doomsday)

Fanon’s text in its breadth of sources, in its innovations in form, and in its interventions into contemporary debates in psychology, psychiatry, history, anthropology, sociology, linguistics, literature and philosophy stands as evidence of its central argument: that Black people are the inheritors of human history as much as any other group. His aim thus far has been to find a new formation of blackness, a search that has led him through the Négritude poets, and to the cul de sac of Sartre’s ‘minor term’. Having then uncovered the foundational motivations of whiteness, Fanon’s strategy going forwards is to abandon the Black and white dualism altogether, and his final chapter is more concerned with the category of human. Due to whiteness’s tendency to masquerade as a universal subject, Fanon is careful to construct his new human category to include the history of the compass, invented in China, as well as what has been part of the founding mythology of whiteness. In so doing, he signals his attempt to formulate the human without its historical exclusions.

In the KMD days, the source material for Dumile’s compositions were a combination of esoteric Black spirituality, Black social life, Black history, and white stereotypes of blackness. He used these to construct and deconstruct notions of blackness. After adopting the mask, however, it is notable how omnivorous his references become, plucked seemingly at random from local radio adverts, pop culture, modern and archaic idioms, and even street signage. He is an archivist of the everyday as much as an obscurantist, and juxtaposes both for a laugh or simply to fit rhymes together. His compositional method is a bricolage; his rhymebooks filled with ‘potential humorous couplets’, according to Ta-Nehisi Coates, which he combines in a way that eschews unity. The range of sources implies a humanism to his writing – that no cultural artefacts are forbidden his appropriation, which applies to the mask itself and its audacious disregard for intellectual property rights. But the variety of sources also serves as an intentional excess: a mask concealing access to a singular notion of his identity that might be betrayed if his sources were to be derived from his contemporary time and place, from high or low culture, or from any one image complex. Beneath the thickly laid impasto of allusions, there are autobiographical details confided in brief, non-sequitous asides: mentioning a friend in prison, losing his fronts, a conversation with his partner, and so on. Each of these instances are hidden among dense lines of bragging raps. Take the song Doomsday, on which the verses leap from the TV show Jeopardy, to nursery rhymes, to west coast slang, and to a dalliance with a woman with a missing tooth. Between these random topics, the chorus finds him in a sombre mood thinking about death and his brother, and the second verse details a stay in a Baltimore prison, yet he flees from these topics almost as soon as he alights upon them. A grattage of James Brown screaming overlays much of the song, giving this disconnected stream of consciousness further noisy obfuscation.

In the opening skit of the album, a voice asks, ’now who will be the first to tell me of a past adventure?’ to which Dumile quietly replies, ‘I ain’t sayin’ nothing’. Unlike the KMD days, a claim to what blackness is or isn’t is no longer central to the project. Instead, Dumile withholds from the white gaze a meaning that can be reduced.

The Tarzan stories, the sagas of twelve-year-old explorers, the adventures of Mickey Mouse, and all those comic books serve actually as a release for collective aggression. The magazines are put together by white men for little white men.
(The Negro and Psychopathology)

What have I done to myself? My face is…hideous! He made me hurry my experiment! Now I must hide my face from all mankind. But he will pay. Oh, how he will pay!
(Doom, are you awake? (skit), Operation:Doomsday)

Somehow the rap game reminds me of the crack game. The central metaphor in the then dominant gangster rap takes the ‘real’ tenor of the artist’s life – the skill required to write raps and the difficulties of navigating the recording industry – and appends it to the vehicle of being a gangster. In this metaphor, lyrics are propelled like bullets; other emcees are rival gang members; disses gangland hits; moving records is moving product; and making money is, well, making money. Operation:Doomsday signifies on this trope and appends the same ‘real’ tenor to the vehicle of superheroes and supervillains, which parodies rap’s central metaphor by bringing attention to the fact that it is a metaphor. Both superhero comics and gangster movies are peculiarly american genres that comment on the american psyche, and both have narratives that revolve around crime, wealth, the individual, and power. Yet it is as ridiculous to imagine that a famous rapper is a gang leader as it is to imagine that they are a comic book villain.

While the use of a persona (literally a mask in Latin) is perhaps the most obvious point of comparison between superheroes and rappers, Dumile also uses origin stories to structure the narrative of the album via its skits, which tell the origin story of Dr. Doom. Hip Hop is likewise replete with origins. The genre itself has a much mythologised origin in DJ Kool Herc’s parties, and this essay has indulged in the origin myth of an antediluvian Hip Hop before the white people flooded in. Individual rappers tend also to cultivate an origin story of themselves before their fame to construct an air of authenticity. It seems a prerequisite that rappers came from nothing, overcame this or that, then struggled against the odds before finding fame. No doubt many did, but such stories affirm notions of meritocracy, social mobility, and the efficacy of individual striving. There is an american-ness to this bootstrapping, but that in the era of the billionaire rapper such stories should persist also suggests proximity to poverty and the allure of a dangerous underclass are important to the scene and its fans, both demanding ‘realness’ from the artists. The origin story of Dumile – his brother’s death and being dropped by his label – is often regarded as animating his entire post-KMD career, so it is notable how elaborately it is withheld. The listener expects it, Dumile teases it, but it is hardly forthcoming until his very last verse on the album. His refusal is both a critique of rappers performing an origin and of fans demanding it.

In his investigation into the psychological origins of the imago, Fanon highlights the importance of children’s comics in the reproduction of white supremacist ideology. He shows how such comics teach superiority to white children, who see characters like themselves as the hero, and inferiority to Black children, who see characters like themselves as villains or savages. This pattern is particularly prominent in colonial regimes built on the genocide of native populations. Teaching children that this was justifiable is vital to the functioning of such societies, which is achieved by making a villain of the displaced population. Fanon proposes, quoting Freud, that collective aggression is sublimated in children’s comics in the same way that repressed thoughts are in a ‘disguise’ as an ersatz thought. This process ensures that the feelings of anger against the perverse economic and social structure are displaced onto the victims rather than the perpetrators of colonial violence. The guilt of benefitting from racial hierarchy slips beneath stories of cowboys and indians, swashbuckling colonial explorers, or white superheroes cleaning up the streets. These comics habituate children to a racist society by rehearsing the narratives of colonisers, answering with falsehoods the questions that children might have about the injustices they see in the world. Their racist images are unifying to whiteness, confirming that which it is not, while strengthening group ties. These comics are not what they seem, and their sublimated messages propagandise the ideology of a racist society. Dumile’s intervention is to identify with the villains of such comics, subverting this ideology and then unmasking the repressed desires of whiteness hidden beneath the panels.

In the white world the man of color encounters difficulties in the development of his bodily schema. Consciousness of the body is solely a negating activity. It is a third-person consciousness.
(The Fact of Blackness)

He’s the villain with the million dollar voice-throw trick,
He’s like a ventriloquist, with his fist in the speaker’s back
(Red and Gold, Operation:Doomsday)

Fanon’s ’third-person consciousness’ shares similarities with WEB Dubois’s double consciousness in which Black americans view themselves from the perspective of white people, as well as from the perspective of other Black people. It is third-person in the sense of seeing oneself from the outside: as ‘he/she/they’ as well as ‘I’. However, the difference in Fanon’s text is the way in which this consciousness is negating because the imposed white gaze disrupts being able to see oneself as an individual, unique ‘I’. Fanon dramatizes this by playing with the possible meanings of ’third-person’. On the train, he exists not singly but triply, occupying three seats as his ‘body’, ‘race’, and ‘ancestors’. This shows the moment of alienation when he becomes racialised and takes on the history and the racist stereotypes the white gaze attaches to this history, but he is not afforded an individual human subjectivity. Despite the alienation of being expelled from the category of human, Fanon proposes in his introduction that being thrust into such a ‘zone of none being’ is a state from which one can begin to imagine new possibilities, where an ‘authentic upheaval can be born’.

Much like his mask, Dumile refers to himself using the third-person ‘he’ as a distancing technique that makes his persona explicit. As well as attributing incriminating actions to the persona, it also redirects the listener away from himself and towards his art. There is not the sense that he is alienated from himself, but instead that he is adopting a position from which he can generate material without giving himself up to the listener and the white gaze. This ‘he’ would become a signature of his raps, but it is used inconsistently on Operation:Doomsday. It was not until the B-side of the third single, Red and Gold, that he began to experiment with it, and even then its first verse uses an ‘I’ pronoun to establish the setting and occasion. Autumn is ‘wig-twisting season’, when people get jumped for their belongings, and the title refers to the changing leaves as well as blood and jewellery. The ‘I’ of the first verse presents an occasion when someone gets mugged before withdrawing it, calling it ‘science fiction’, an ‘absurd tale’, and blinding like an ’eclipse’. In the second verse, the third-person takes over: another persona in King Geedorah. The King Geedorah character is a space-monster lifted from the Godzilla franchise, who speaks through MF DOOM on earth hence the description of him as a ‘ventriloquist’. When King Geedorah speaks, he offers a few disparate images, taking the listener away from the mugging incident as Dumile withdraws access to autobiography.

Dumile then changes persona again. Sci.Fly, a character who also appears on Go with the Flow and The Finest, is only ever deployed for eight bars and tends to offer what seems to be an incriminating reveal, which then disappears into metaphor. His name and how he is described on Go with the Flow as ‘quick to whip up a script like Rod Serling’, imply his stories are fictional. The lines ‘Me myself, I got flavors that out-taste most/With numb gums, some rhymers is lactose’ might suggest that he deals cocaine, but the words ‘flavors’ and ‘rhymers’ presents it also as a metaphor, that his rhymes are like pure cocaine, while other rappers’ rhymes are cut. Similarly, on Red and Gold, Sci.Fly is deployed to deliver the line ‘Got bagged ‘cause of the dirty chick with make-up/Bail out quick ‘for the 7:30 wake-up’, which could be a reference to Dumile being arrested, naming who was at fault, and how he was released after only one night in jail, possibly relating to an event in Dumile’s life when he was arrested on a drug charge. However, the line could equally suggest he wore a condom, ‘bagged’, to have sex with a ‘dirty chick’, then left before her alarm rang. Dumile understands that the audience expects a confessional mode in Hip Hop, but Sci.fly is used on Operation:Doomsday to deliver an enticing performance, like a magic trick, performed to the inquisitive white gaze. Ultimately, the promise of a confession or autobiographical detail draws the listener in, but then is made to disappear, the ‘odd merlin’ along with it. On Operation:Doomsday, Dumile introduced the mask, the third-person, and multiple personas as strategies of fugitivity so as not to offer to the white gaze a clear look at Black life. They allow plausible deniability. Then on later albums the use of ‘I’ all but disappears, and access with it.

Good-Evil, Beauty-Ugliness, White-Black: such are the characteristic pairings of the phenomenon that, […] we shall call ‘manicheism delirium.’
(The Negro and Psychopathology)

A villain in your land, in his land a ruler.
(?, Operation:Doomsday)

Fanon’s concept of manicheism delirium illuminates another way that Dumile uses comic books to critique society. Fanon makes his remarks in the middle of a consideration of Sartre’s writing on antisemitism, which Sartre says is animated by a scapegoat morality. White middle-class society is rigid and closed, and it polices its boundaries by casting things outside it and making a devil of it. As a response, Dumile’s villainous persona preempts this aspect of middle-class morality, embracing villainy in the form of deviant social behaviour such as drug dealing and infidelity. These can be read as deviations from a normative white centre from which blackness can’t but deviate. He also critiques institutions such as schools that ‘drop jewels’ and ‘TV News that’s designed to fool you’, and in this sense there is righteousness in being an unjust society’s villain. Within the structure of comic books, the villain upsets the equilibrium in place at the story’s beginning, and it is the thwarting of the villain at the end of the story that sets things back to normal again. The villain’s role is anti-social, challenging mainstream social mores, and in this way they are the characters in comics that can create change. By analogy, Dumile positions himself as an outsider who can challenge mainstream Hip Hop and mainstream america.

The translation of Black and white to villain and hero underscores that the grand narrative of american culture is a melodrama of warring dualisms. Nuance is banished and the world is split into cowboys and Indians, capitalists and communists, citizens and immigrants, and so on. Dumile seems at first to adopt the manicheism delirium of racial thinking by accepting the attribution of a villain in ‘your land’, but in ‘his’ land he interrupts the dualism by not calling himself a hero, but instead a ‘ruler’. These ’lands’ are ambiguous. At one level they are Dr. Doom’s kingdom of Latveria set against Mr. Fantastic’s america, but otherwise they could suggest a number of dualisms: mainstream and underground Hip Hop, licit and illicit economies, white america and Black america. Whether the listener is Black or white, Dumile keeps his distance so that it could also be a dualism of his public self and private. According to Robin DG Kelley, Hip Hop conventionally uses the first-person to speak to collective experience. Any ‘I’ is often a persona to create social-realist narratives of life in the inner cities rather than simply the perspective of the artists themselves. When speaking with Mr. Hood on KMD’s first album, Dumile uses the first-person plural in ‘we built this place’ and used the character of Mr. Hood as a cipher to give advice and knowledge to his Black listeners. Writing in the first-person plural (us) instates solidarity, and a corollary third-person plural (them) reifies a common adversary. On Operation:Doomsday this becomes the ‘he/him’ and sometimes ‘I/me’ of Dumile against the ‘you’ of the audience and everyone else. If a dualism exists on Operation:Doomsday, it is Dumile against the world.

There is a quest for the Negro, the Negro is in demand. One cannot get along without him, he is needed, but only if he is made palatable in a certain way.
(The Negro and Psychopathology)

He cleans his metal mask with gasoline, they after him.
(?, Operation:Doomsday)

Fanon attributes the popularity of best-sellers that depict the ‘racial problem’ to the racist white imago of blackness. Seeing the imago reproduced gives credence to it (they are all like that); seeing it undermined gives a masochistic, guilt-purging pleasure (they aren’t all like that, how enlightened of me to notice). To the white gaze, even the Br’er rabbit stories are turned from subversive trickster narratives that challenge power into proof of the Black man’s proximity to animals. The presence of the imago corrupts every encounter, but inasmuch as the white gaze is stupefied by the imago, it persists in demanding representations of blackness because they help it to imagine the other, while also helping it to constitute itself against the other.

As well as the Fantastic Four skits of Operation:Doomsday being good versus evil origin stories, they also follow the comic book convention of being a pursuit. Throughout a typical comic, the hero is one step behind the villain until the last pages when the hero finally gets the upper hand. There is the overhanging threat of unmasking, but in the album’s skits Dr. Doom escapes after his plans are foiled, inevitably to return in a later episode. Dr. Doom is presented as a fugitive, disappearing half way through the album and exploding without trace at the end. Although The Fantastic Four give chase and feel as though they have triumphed at the end of the album, they must content themselves with not entirely overcoming Dr. Doom. The whole story is not told from Dr. Doom’s perspective but instead by people who walk in on him, disturb him, and sabotage his efforts. This dynamic between Dr. Doom and the Fantastic Four can be seen as an analogy of Dumile’s relation to the recording industry that shelved his album and dropped KMD, as well as his fans. Their acquisitive, consuming white gaze searches him out, trying to discover his origins, his story, and to feed upon his struggle and grief. What is taken when a performer commits their very self to a performance is a central concern in Hip Hop, and Dumile uses one of its foundational texts, Wild Style, to introduce this theme. The opening skit samples a conversation from the film, asking whether fame, or having a ‘picture in the paper’, leads to getting ‘money like Barry White’, but the protagonist Zoro’s reluctance shows that when entering the limelight there is more at stake. Dumile’s work has adopted different strategies to deal with the epistemic violence of the white gaze. He refutes the imago of blackness that attends the white gaze in the first KMD album, embraces it in the second, but on Operation:Doomsday assigning a stable imago to what is hidden, heterogeneous, and fugitive is an impossible task. By concealing himself, Dumile makes the listener feel themself chasing after him, and may make the white listener ask: what am I looking for from this? What desires are being satisfied by being a consumer of Hip Hop, a genre in many ways signalled as not being for me? What imagos of blackness attend my gaze?

Dumile’s mask is made of reflective metal, which can be understood as forcing the white gaze to look back at itself.

At the conclusion of this study, I want the world to recognise, with me, the open door of every consciousness.
My final prayer: o my body, make of me always a man who questions.
(By way of conclusion)

Don’t question how we live, question what we give
Take it to the next, leave ’em where they can’t see me
(? Feat. Kurious, Operation:Doomsday)

Dumile’s first three records each feature a song with a question in the title. Who me? from Mr. Hood preempts the white listener’s imago of blackness; What a N***** know? from BL_CK B_ST_RDS sees the group performing an exaggeration of the imago to mock the white gaze; but ? from Operation:Doomsday is a little more mysterious because the content of the question is missing, and only the mark remains. It at first seems taken from lines spoken by Jorge Alvarez AKA Kurious guesting on the track, which is a plea to the listener not to ask questions or observe them. However, another reading of this question mark comes in Dumile’s verse when he is mistaken for his deceased brother, ’these hoes be asking me if I’m you’. This case of mistaken identity hearkens back to the KMD records when he is mistaken for the group’s logo, or when ‘Black bastards’ was misattributed to the group. These other instances show the error-prone operations of the white gaze, but being mistaken for his brother, to have his lineaments ever-present on his skin, suggests that the mask hides him from himself, a reading that does not centre the white gaze in the album’s construction. Dumile’s verse on the song is a poignant 16-bar tribute to his brother, written in couplets without a single device deployed to render it ambiguous, making it unlike any other verse on the album. Dumile delivers a montage of images:

Like my twin brother, we did everything together
From hundred raka’at salats to copping butter leathers
Remember when you went and got the dark blue Ballys?
I had all the different color Cazals and Gazelles
The ‘SUBROC’ three-finger ring with the ruby in the ‘O’, akh
Truly the illest dynamic duo on the whole block
I keep a flick of you with the machete sword in your hand
Everything is going according to plan, man.

These images are a chronology of the siblings’ lives. Shared spirituality, shared emulation of rappers in their youth, finding fame together, and appearing in music videos. That the sequence begins with images of the pair but ends on Subroc alone with a machete symbolizes the brothers’ separation. The studium of these images is available to the listener, but the punctum is in their particularity – what these particular garments meant to them, the particular meaning of the ring, and the particular story behind the machete image. This is not available. As images, they cannot be appropriated for an imago because they are personal, specific, and cannot be incorporated into notions of blackness generally. The ‘you’ used elsewhere to refer to the listener is now directed towards his brother, inscribing a private relation that cuts out the listener. His final line follows on from BL_CK B_ST_RDS’s ending: a promise between the two brothers is alluded to, but once more is not revealed to the listener.

The dynamic between white and Black that Fanon has elaborated throughout Black Skin, White Masks is defined by inequality. By not being understood as equal, there is no chance for recognition, so questions of superiority and inferiority hinder each’s ability to see and understand the other. What Fanon wants, by the end, is for all the world to recognise. That is to say, to be in a state where recognition is possible by accepting that all humans are equal. To achieve this state, a new definition of the ‘human’ needs to be formulated. There are a number of ways that Fanon admits the inadequacy of his conclusion, not least by titling it En guise de conclusion, rendered By way of conclusion in the English translations. There is in these concluding remarks an outline of what needs to be done, but it is notable that he does not address whiteness, at least not directly. Instead the final address is to his own body. Spatially and temporally immediate, his own body is what he himself can change, and an appeal to it is also to the actually existing. In this way, he ends on a concession; what feels like a defeat, but it is at least a position from which he can ask questions. Though it is clear what the other must do, he cannot make the other do it. He can move himself towards a new humanism by being curious about the world and the other, but he cannot make the white world abandon its imago and commit to the same.

In this there is perhaps one final way of understanding the book’s title. In the culmination of the face-to-face motif, Fanon does away with masks and stands before the white other, open and exposed. He comes to the encounter in a state of vulnerability, but also sensitivity and without preconceptions, and invites whiteness to do the same. Until that happens, there are two sides of the encounter. Black skin: White masks. The noun ‘masks’ becomes a verb. Fanon’s book tells the story of him removing the masks whiteness creates for blackness, then removing those which blackness creates for itself in order to set the stage for an encounter where each can see the other in their fullness. He imagines a world without racialised imagos; where reciprocal recognition is possible, though the onus is on white people to abandon their imagos. Dumile, by contrast, is not willing to expose himself in the racially ordered world, so he instead wears a mask and withdraws from the white gaze, but not before deploying a series of strategies to force whiteness to become conscious of its own looking. Armed with his villainous, consciousness-raising strategies, he plans to one day put an end to the racially ordered world and bring about Doomsday.

Ha Ha, Get it…? Doom…? Doomsday?
Yeah, we get it – oh, brother!
(?, Operation:Doomsday)
